## The Missing Piece

Painters leave behind finished canvases. Bakers turn flour into bread. Mechanics fix the engine, and the car drives away.

Architects create blueprints — drawings that, without enormous capital and an army of contractors, do absolutely nothing. They simply gather dust on a shelf.

That always left me with a sense of incompleteness. I couldn't bring things to life by myself. It felt wrong.

That's one of the reasons why programming felt so alluring.

There, things were different.

## Some May Disagree

Some might argue that software engineering is no different. That you still need massive FAANG-level teams and enormous amounts of capital to build anything meaningful.

Some might even go a step further and say that architecture and software engineering are almost identical at a meta level. You analyze the problem, choose the right technology and structure, then translate your solution into a precise engineering language — white lines or symbols on a black canvas.

Frankly, they're not wrong.

But they're missing the point.

Code is not a blueprint.

## A Blueprint That Comes Alive

Imagine an architect walking onto an empty construction site, throwing the blueprints onto the ground... and watching an entire building assemble itself. That's exactly what code does on a server. It follows the instructions, becomes ready, fully functional, alive.

That's why programming felt so different to me. As an architect, I designed buildings that depended on hundreds of people before they could exist. As a software engineer, I also build them myself.

`That's the difference I've been looking for.`
